Exploring Uranium Glass

Glassware that contains uranium compounds, glowing with brilliant green fluorescence under UV light. A safe, fascinating intersection of art, chemistry, and atomic-age history.

Uranium content:
0.1% – 2% by weight
Peak production:
1920s – 1930s
Revival:
1958 – present

What Is It?

Chemistry

Uranium glass is glass that has been colored with uranium compounds — typically uranium dioxide (UO₂) or sodium diuranate — added directly to the molten glass mixture. The uranium content usually ranges from 0.1% to 2% by weight, though rare historical pieces have contained up to 25% uranium oxide.

The uranium serves a dual purpose: it provides distinctive colors in normal light (yellows, greens, and ambers) and produces the famous bright green fluorescence under ultraviolet (UV) light. The exact color depends on the oxidation state of the uranium and the presence of other elements in the glass formula.

The Science of Fluorescence

Physics

Uranium atoms in the glass matrix absorb invisible ultraviolet radiation and re-emit it as visible light in the green spectrum. This fluorescence is a physical property of the uranium ions and is completely independent of the glass's radioactivity — even pieces with very low uranium content will fluoresce strongly.

Normal Light

Under ordinary lighting, uranium glass appears as a pale yellow-green, straw yellow, or light amber. Collectors often describe its surface as having a slightly oily or greasy luster — the characteristic that gave "Vaseline glass" its name.

UV Light

Under a black light, the transformation is striking. The glass emits a luminous, fluorescent green glow ranging from bright yellow-green to deep neon green — the unmistakable signature that makes identification instant and reliable.

Colors & Varieties

Types
Color Common Name Notes
Yellow-Green Vaseline Glass The most iconic variety; transparent with an oily luster. Named for its resemblance to petroleum jelly.
Green Annagrün Popularized by Josef Riedel in the 1830s. A deeper green hue created by uranium in an oxidized state.
Yellow Annagelb / Canary Glass Also Riedel's creation; bright canary yellow. Sometimes called "canary glass" after the color of the bird.
Blue Uranium Blue Extremely rare. Uranium combined with other colorants to achieve a blue tone.
Amber Uranium Amber Higher concentrations or different oxidation states produce warm amber tones.
Opalescent Pearline Glass Davidson's invention (1889) with white opalescent edges graduating to transparent yellow or blue.

Note: Vaseline glass is technically a subset of uranium glass — specifically, the transparent yellow to yellow-green variety. Not all uranium glass is Vaseline glass, though the terms are often used interchangeably by casual collectors.

History

Timeline

1817 — First Documented Reference

The earliest known written reference to uranium glass appears, though production was still experimental and limited.

1834 — Riedel Popularizes Uranium Glass

Bohemian glassmaker Josef Riedel develops Annagelb (yellow) and Annagrün (green), named after the Anna Valley in Czechia. These become the first commercially successful uranium glass lines.

1889 — Davidson Invents Pearline Glass

British manufacturer George Davidson & Co. introduces Pearline glass — pressed glass with a white opalescent rim graduating to transparent yellow or blue. It becomes a Victorian sensation.

1920s–1930s — Art Deco Boom

Production explodes during the Art Deco era. American companies like Fenton, Hocking, Indiana Glass, and Federal Glass mass-produce tableware, vases, and decorative items. Much of this overlaps with Depression Glass.

Early 1940s — World War II Shutdown

Uranium becomes a strictly regulated strategic material for the Manhattan Project. Civilian glassmakers worldwide lose access to uranium oxide, and production halts almost overnight.

1958 — De-regulation & Revival

Uranium is de-regulated, allowing glassmakers to resume production. Fenton and others produce uranium glass again, though in smaller quantities than the pre-war boom.

Sunlight Inspection Preliminary check Some pieces show a faint greenish fluorescence in strong sunlight, but this is less reliable.

Is It Safe?

Yes — with important caveats. Uranium glass emits alpha, beta, and small amounts of gamma radiation, but the levels are extremely low. Alpha particles cannot penetrate a sheet of paper or human skin and are fully blocked by the glass itself. The total radiation exposure from handling uranium glass is less than the natural background radiation you receive daily.

⚠

Do Not Use for Food or Drink

While handling is safe, eating or drinking from uranium glass is not recommended — especially if the piece is scratched or chipped. Uranium can potentially leach into acidic foods or beverages, and uranium is chemically toxic if ingested (more toxic than lead). Enjoy uranium glass as a display item.

Collector Tips

Advice
1

Carry a UV Flashlight

The glow is immediate and unmistakable. Be aware that some non-uranium modern green glass can look similar in normal light.

2

Authenticate the Age

Look for mold marks, pontil scars on hand-blown pieces, and wear patterns consistent with age. Learn known pattern names.

3

Know the Makers

Key names include Fenton, Davidson, Sowerby, Greener, Riedel, Federal, Hocking, and Indiana Glass. Signed pieces command premiums.

4

Inspect Condition Carefully

Chips, cracks, and scratches significantly reduce value. Avoid pieces with extensive surface wear or cloudiness.

5

Beware of Reproductions

Since 1958, many patterns have been reproduced. Reproductions are genuine uranium glass but lack the historical value of pre-war pieces.

6

Display with UV Lighting

Install UV LED strips in display cabinets to show off the fluorescence. Keep pieces away from prolonged direct sunlight to preserve color.

7

Handle Minimally

Oils from hands, temperature shocks, and abrasive cleaning can damage antique glass. Wash gently by hand when necessary.

Value & Rarity

Market

Uranium glass can range from $5 for common Depression Glass pieces to hundreds or thousands of dollars for rare Victorian Art Glass, signed pieces by notable makers, or unusual colors and forms. The key factors affecting value are:

  • Age — Pre-1940s pieces are generally more valuable.
  • Manufacturer & Pattern Rarity — Some patterns were produced in very limited runs.
  • Color — Blue uranium glass is particularly rare and desirable.
  • Condition — No chips, cracks, or repairs. Original surface finish is critical.
  • Form — Figural pieces, elaborate vases, and novelties command higher prices than common tableware.
