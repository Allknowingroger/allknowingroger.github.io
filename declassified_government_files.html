Navigating the Government's Secret Files

How the Defense Intelligence Agency processes Freedom of Information Act requests, what hides inside the Electronic Reading Room, and where documented history ends and urban legend begins.

The Process

Five Steps from Secret to Public

The 1996 amendments to FOIA mandate publicly accessible "electronic reading rooms" with agency response materials. Here is how a request moves from intake to release.

1
Initial Processing

Intake and Registration

Requests submitted to the DIA FOIA Service Center are logged and assigned a case number. This creates a tracking record, allowing the agency to manage workflow, estimate backlogs, and maintain accountability throughout what can be a multi-month process.

2
Records Search & Review

Locating the Data

Agency personnel conduct a comprehensive search across physical and digital archives. This includes not only formal reports but also emails, memoranda, briefing slides, and other internal communications. The scope depends on the specificity of the request.

3
SME and Consultative Review

Expert Analysis and Referral

Subject Matter Experts examine retrieved documents for sensitivity. If a document touches on the jurisdiction of another agency—such as the CIA, NSA, or an armed service—it is "referred" to that agency for its own review. This interagency coordination is a major reason complex requests can take years.

4
Quality Control & Redaction

Protecting Sensitive Secrets

Records are reviewed line-by-line. Material that falls under one of FOIA's nine specific exemption categories—such as classified national security information, intelligence sources and methods, or personal privacy—is . This step balances transparency with the legal obligation to protect ongoing operations.

5
Delivery

Releasing to the Public

Once processing is complete, final records are delivered to the requester and simultaneously posted to the DIA's Electronic Reading Room. This ensures the information is available not just to the original requester, but to journalists, historians, and the general public without requiring duplicate requests.

Inside the Reading Room

Record Categories

The DIA's public reading room contains a wide spectrum of intelligence products—some routine, others at the center of public fascination.

🚀

Advanced Aerospace Weapon Systems

Includes records from AAWSAP and AATIP, DIA programs initiated in 2008 to research cutting-edge and speculative aerospace technologies. Released materials include contract briefings and Defense Intelligence Reference Documents covering advanced propulsion and related concepts.

🛸

Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena

Materials on phenomena tracked across space, air, land, sea, and undersea environments. Reflects the modern military framing of UAP as an "all-domain" issue, with records continuing to fuel public and congressional interest.

🌍

Regional Threat Centers

Analytical products for the China Mission Group, Americas & Transregional Threat Center, and Europe-Eurasia Regional Center. Declassified assessments help researchers understand the DIA's global intelligence posture.

📁

Specialized Dossiers

Non-traditional records including medical and biological threat assessments, POW records, interrogation reports, and Government Accountability Office investigations—illustrating that FOIA releases cover far more than military hardware.

Historic Investigation

Project Blue Book (1947–1969)

Project Blue Book was the U.S. Air Force's systematic study of unidentified flying objects, operating from 1952 until its termination in December 1969. Official National Archives records state the Air Force investigated 12,618 sightings under the program.

12,618 Official UFO Reports Investigated
  • 1952Project Blue Book formally established as the Air Force's systematic UFO study.

    Replaced earlier projects Sign and Grudge.

  • 1968Internal review concludes no UFO posed a national security threat.

    The project is deemed no longer justifiable on national security or scientific grounds.

  • 1969Official termination of Project Blue Book in December.

    All investigations cease; remaining unexplained cases are archived.

  • 1976Records microfilmed and transferred to NARA.

    The complete case files enter the public custody of the National Archives.

Notable Case

Minot AFB, October 1968

Case #12,548 contains approximately 146 pages of data, radar logs, and B-52 bomber-related photographs. It remains one of the most extensively documented sightings from the late period of Project Blue Book.

Archive

Public Access Today

Project Blue Book records are available in NARA Record Group 341. Many case files have been digitized and are accessible online through the National Archives catalog.

Fact vs. Fiction

The Philadelphia Experiment Denied

The boundary between documented history and popular myth is often what declassification efforts seek to clarify. The Philadelphia Experiment is one of the most enduring examples of the latter.

The Allegation

According to conspiracy lore, in October 1943 the U.S. Navy conducted an experiment at the Philadelphia Naval Shipyard to make the destroyer escort USS Eldridge (DE-173) invisible to radar—or even teleport it. The story claims sailors were fused into bulkheads, driven insane, or vanished entirely.

The Official Position

"The Office of Naval Research has never conducted any investigations on invisibility." — Naval History and Heritage Command / Office of Naval Research

The ONR and Naval Historical Center have consistently and explicitly denied the story. The Navy considers the entire episode to be a product of science fiction and misinformation, with no basis in actual naval research.

Origin

Where the Myth Came From

The story surfaced in the 1950s through letters from Carl M. Allen, then spread through books and a 1984 film. Researchers trace it to misunderstandings of wartime degaussing—a real technology used to make ships less vulnerable to magnetic mines.

Verdict

No Credible Documentation

No credible documentation of such an experiment exists in declassified naval records. The USS Eldridge's actual deck logs show it was elsewhere in the Atlantic on the dates in question.
